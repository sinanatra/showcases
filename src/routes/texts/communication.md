# Communication Policies

A comparison of police reports published by Berlin and Brandenburg state police departments reveals notable deviations between the two federal states. That invites an analysis of variations in the selection of right-wing extremist crimes and in individual reporting styles, in order to reflect on the public-relations cultures within state police departments and on structural differences between the authorities. 

Of all reported incidents, 528 cases (24,5% of the dataset) refer to the painting of a swastika (“Hakenkreuz”). The vast majority of these crimes (485) are reported in Brandenburg, while only 43 cases appear in Berlin police reports. By contrast, of the 173 reports (8%) addressing antisemitism, most (150) were published by Berlin police, and only 23 by Brandenburg. A similar pattern of selective visibility appears in the reporting on LGBTQI-related offenses. Of the 247 (11,5%) homo-, trans- and queerphobic cases in the dataset, only three are documented in Brandenburg. 

Of the 611 cases (28,4%) mentioning a xenophobic motivation, 504 are registered in Berlin, while 107 occurred in Brandenburg. In Brandenburg, many cases refer to incidents that took place in the past and were later reported to the police, without direct intervention by officers, as the following example shows:

“On Friday, a complaint of verbal abuse was filed with the police. On Ascension Day, a 56-year-old man was jogging in the Boblitz area. During a break, the man, who is from Zimbabwe, was subjected to xenophobic abuse by a group of teenagers on bicycles. The criminal investigation department has taken over further investigations.” 

Comparable incidents also occur in Berlin. However, the Berlin police department is more likely to report cases involving direct intervention by police officers, as the following example demonstrates: 

“A woman was arrested yesterday afternoon in Prenzlauer Berg after making xenophobic remarks. According to initial findings, the 56-year-old woman is alleged to have verbally abused four children aged 11 and 12 who were playing on Arnswalder Platz at around 5:35 p.m. and to have made xenophobic insults toward one of them.” 

Differences in reporting on swastika graffiti, antisemitism, queerphobia, and xenophobia between the Berlin and Brandenburg police departments point to divergent communicative practices and institutional cultures. In Berlin, swastika graffiti, which remains a central symbol of right-wing extremist activity, is largely absent from police reports. Conversely, the near absence of police reports addressing homo-, trans- and queerphobic crimes in Brandenburg renders the presence and experiences of LGBTQI people in the state largely invisible. Brandenburg reports more often document crimes reported after the fact or cases with unknown suspects, such as the discovery of swastika graffiti.

Beyond case selection, the language used in police reports reveals biased deviations, stylistic variations, and structural differences within the public-relations departments. All Berlin cases are issued by one central office, producing a largely standardized voice across reports, as illustrated by the following example:

“Yesterday at noon, officers from Police Precinct 44 arrested a man in Tempelhof who is alleged to have attacked a woman. [...] As there is suspicion that the attack was motivated by xenophobia, the State Security Division of the State Criminal Police Office has taken over further investigations into the case of aggravated assault.” 

Berlin police reports typically include a precise classification of politically motivated crime and explicitly state when the State Security Division has taken over investigations. Only twelve Berlin reports specify a motivation in this manner, as in the example above. In one case, the motivation is not explicitly named, which is notable given that the suspect is a police officer himself:

“In connection with the sending of a text message with right-wing extremist content by a law enforcement officer, a specialist unit of the State Security Police has been conducting investigations since last Friday. [...] The motive for the crime and the circumstances surrounding it are now being clarified by the specialist unit of the State Criminal Police Office.” 

In Brandenburg, press releases are coordinated by four regional police departments – North, South, West and East. In reporting on right-wing extremist crimes, the Eastern Police Department is by far the most active and uses the most standardized language. It is also the only department that frequently employs the term ‘motivation’ in its reports. However, similar to the previously discussed Berlin exception, the definition of motivation is frequently deferred as part of ongoing investigations, even in cases with an obvious antisemitic context, as the following examples illustrate:

“Police officers on patrol in Schulstraße noticed that graffiti artists had been vandalizing a wall surrounding the Jewish cemetery. The perpetrators had written a code number that's used in the right-wing scene as a greeting from the Nazi era. The criminal investigation department is now trying to figure out who did it and what their motivation was.” 

“Unknown persons left Nazi symbols and anti-Semitic graffiti at a bus stop in Neu-Lindenberg. The graffiti was removed immediately after evidence was secured. The State Security Division of the Eastern Police Department is now investigating who was responsible and what their motives were.”

The reports further reveal an intransparent discontinuity in the transfer of investigative responsibilities to the State Security Division. In the area state Brandenburg, this is also due to the fact that not all decentrally organized police departments have comparable access to State Security units. As a result, many cases in Brandenburg initially involve the criminal police and are only later passed on to the State Security Division. Compared to Berlin, this practice suggests a lower level of urgency in the communicative framing of right-wing extremist crimes. This imbalance also becomes evident in the language use, as demonstrated by the following example from the Southern Police Department of Brandenburg: 


“The supposed football fans on Weinbergstraße have fulfilled every stereotype. Four vehicles were vandalized in a parking lot here on Tuesday night. [...] The whole thing was “garnished” with swastikas, so that now the police state security service is also investigating.”  

The language used in this report is colloquial and verbal embellishments and quotation marks used in reference to swastika graffiti suggest a trivialization of the crime. This framing is part of a broader reporting pattern related to football fans, which points toward a contemporary banalization of evil: 

“Witnesses noticed that unknown persons had “embellished” the facade. In addition to a swastika, there were also fan declarations for a local soccer club in red paint.” 

Comparing press reports issued by the Berlin and Brandenburg police departments does not allow conclusions about the actual prevalence of specific crimes or about policing practices in the field. Nonetheless, examining which incidents police departments select to be showcased in public communication offers insights into how institutional cultures may be structured. Unfolding the tonal peculiarities and linguistic patterns in these reports helps us to illuminate how right-wing extremist activity is evaluated and which notions about such crimes are conveyed through police communication.
